# Ashfall: 12 Days Survival Under a Volcano (Part 1)
*Long version: about 10 minutes, about 1,450 words. Narrator: calm, serious, talks to "you". Covers story bible beats 1 to 8.*

---

## COLD OPEN

If you ever hear a roar coming down a dry riverbed, don't stop to look. Run uphill.

**[DAY 60]**

The ground under your boots starts to hum.
Your little dog won't move. His ears are straight up.
Behind you, a grey wall is pouring down the valley, and it's eating the trees.

This is the story of one hundred days under a volcano.
And of a dog who knew things before you did.
It starts on an ordinary evening.

---

## DAY 1

**[DAY 1]**

Six twelve in the evening. Ember Valley.
A quiet farm town with one road, one river, and one mountain.
Mount Grael has slept for six hundred years. Kids climb it on school trips.

You're making soup when the plates on your shelf begin to rattle.
Then the windows. Then the floor.
Out the kitchen window, the top of the mountain is glowing orange.

Every phone in town buzzes at once.
"Volcanic eruption. Ash fall expected. Stay indoors."

Across the road, your neighbours throw bags into their car.
Doors slam. Tyres spin. They're gone.
But they left something behind.
A small brown dog with one floppy ear, sitting alone in the middle of the road, watching the car disappear.

You open your door and whistle once.
He runs straight in and hides under the table.
You call him Pebble.

**First problem: the air.**

Ash rides the wind. It slips in through open windows, through chimneys, through cracks under doors.
Even a kitchen fan will suck it inside.
So you close every window and lock it.
You shut the flue in the chimney.
You switch off every fan in the house.
Then you soak old towels and roll them along the bottom of every outside door.
Your house is a sealed box now.

Nine forty. Something grey drifts past the porch light.
Soft. Slow. Like the first snow of winter.
It isn't snow.
You open the door one inch and push a little onto your hand.
It doesn't melt. It doesn't feel soft.
It feels like sand. And when you rub it, it squeaks.

Volcanic ash isn't like the ash from a campfire.
It's tiny pieces of rock and volcanic glass, smaller than flour.
It scratches. It cuts. It ruins engines.
And tonight, it's falling on everything you own.

**Second problem: water.**

Your house gets water from a tank and a rain barrel.
By tomorrow, both will be full of grey grit.
So tonight, while the water is still clean, you fill everything.
Every bottle. Every pot. The big soup pan. The bathroom bucket. Pebble's bowl.
One person needs about a gallon of water a day, for drinking, cooking and washing.
You count. Thirty-one gallons.
That's about a month, if you're careful.

Then you go out once more, with your yellow raincoat zipped to your chin.
You put the lid on the rain barrel, and a heavy stone on top.
Back inside. Door shut. Towel down.

That night, the ash hisses on the roof like rain that never stops.
And at ten o'clock, Pebble climbs onto the windowsill.
He doesn't look at the mountain.
He looks the other way, up at the dark ridge above town.
And he whines. Softly. For an hour.

---

## DAY 2

**[DAY 2]**

Seven in the morning. You open the curtains, and the world is gone.
Grey roofs. Grey trees. A grey sky with no sun in it.
No birds. No cars. No sound at all.

You lean a ruler against the outside of the window glass.
Two centimetres.

Then you feel a cold draught.
The back door is open a crack.
Pebble has pushed it with his nose.
He's out in the yard, sneezing, rolling in the ash like it's snow.

Rule two: never go back outside for anything.
You go anyway.

The ash hits your face. It gets behind your glasses.
Your eye starts to burn, and your hand goes up to rub it.
Don't.
Ash in your eye is like sand on a window. Rubbing it scratches.
You stumble inside, tip your head back, and pour clean water over your eye until it stops stinging.

You hold your glasses up to the light.
A tiny scratch, right in the middle of the left lens.
You take them off, and the room turns into a blur.
You can't read the ruler. You can barely see your own hand.
Your glasses are the most important thing you own now.

So you make a promise.
Glasses on. Always. And never contact lenses in ash. A speck of glass under a contact lens can scratch your eye badly.

Then you kneel down with an old towel and wipe Pebble's paws, one by one.
If ash stays in his fur, he'll lick it off and swallow it.
He holds still and lets you do it.
Then he licks your hand.

---

## DAY 4

**[DAY 4]**

Thirty gallons. Six centimetres.

At noon, the lights flicker. Once. Twice.
Then a bang somewhere down the road, and everything goes silent.
The fridge stops humming. The clock on the oven goes dark.

Ash is heavy, and when it gets damp, it carries electricity.
It piles on the power lines, and it shorts them out.

You pull out a flashlight and a box of batteries.
You count them on the table. Fourteen.
From now on, the flashlight is only for emergencies.
In the daytime, you sit by the window and use the grey light.

**Third problem: the fridge.**
A closed fridge stays cold for about four hours.
A full freezer, about two days.
So today, you eat the fridge first. Cheese, milk, leftover soup.
Pebble gets the sausages. He thinks this is the best day of his life.

At ten o'clock, he's back on the windowsill, staring up at the ridge.
You look too.
For one second, you think you see something up there.
Then it's gone.

---

## DAY 9

**[DAY 9]**

Twenty-four gallons. Nine centimetres. Eleven batteries.

You wake up coughing.
The house is sealed, but every time Pebble goes out to the toilet, a little ash comes back in.
It's in the carpet. In the blankets. In the air you breathe.

The workshop is through the side door, joined to the house.
You find your grandfather's old toolbox and, under it, a box of dust masks. The good kind. The kind that fit tight around your nose and mouth.
You count them. Twelve.

You could tie a wet cloth over your face instead.
But a wet cloth only helps a little. Ash is so fine it slips right through.
A mask that fits close to your face stops far more of it.

You put one on. You breathe.
Twelve masks. If you only wear one when you clean or go outside, they might last.

You mist the ash on the floor with a spray bottle before you sweep it.
Dry ash flies up. Damp ash stays down.
Pebble sneezes anyway.

---

## DAY 12

**[DAY 12]**

Twenty gallons. Ten centimetres. Eleven masks.

Ten centimetres of ash weighs more than you think.
It sits on every roof in Ember Valley, and it doesn't blow away.

That night, lying in bed, you hear it.
A long, slow creak. Right above your head.
Then a tick. Then another.

You want to climb up there with a shovel and push it all off.
Don't.
Ash is slippery, like walking on marbles.
After eruptions, falling off a roof is one of the most common ways people get hurt.

So you do the safe thing.
You shine the flashlight at the ceiling.
No sagging. No cracks. Not yet.
You make one more rule.
If the ceiling ever starts to bend, you leave that room, and you don't go back.

Pebble isn't on the bed tonight.
He's in the doorway, half in the room and half in the hallway.
Watching the ceiling.
